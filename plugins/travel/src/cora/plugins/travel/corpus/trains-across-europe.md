# Trains across Europe — what I have learned booking them

## Booking windows

Most operators open sales three to six months ahead, and the cheapest fares are the
earliest ones. Advance fares are tied to one train and are not refundable; the flexible
fare costs roughly double and can be moved.

Night trains are the exception: berths sell out long before seats, and a sleeper booked
two weeks out is usually gone or expensive.

## Reservations

A pass is not a ticket on every train. High-speed and night services almost always
require a seat or berth reservation on top of the pass, and the reservation quota for
pass holders is small. Regional trains generally need none — turn up and board.

## Changing trains

Allow an hour for a connection between operators in a large station, and twenty minutes
within one. A missed connection on a single through-ticket is the operator's problem; two
separately booked tickets make it yours.

## Luggage

There is no weight limit and no check-in. Racks at the carriage ends take large cases,
the overhead shelf takes cabin-sized ones. Nothing is tagged, so anything valuable stays
with you.

## Comfort

Seats facing the direction of travel are quieter. Coaches next to the restaurant car see
constant traffic. On double-deckers the upper deck is the calmer one.

## Costs, roughly

A long domestic leg booked early runs a fraction of the walk-up price. A night train with
a couchette compares to a mid-range hotel room, and saves a day of travelling.
